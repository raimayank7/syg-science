The periodic table: an introduction

How the elements are arranged by atomic number into periods and groups, and the trends this reveals.

Classes 9 and 10Chemistry505 words

There are 118 known elements. Learning the properties of each one separately would be hopeless. Chemists therefore looked for a pattern, a way to arrange the elements so that similar ones fall together. The result is the periodic table, one of the most useful charts in all of science.

Early attempts

  • Dobereiner noticed groups of three similar elements, which he called triads, such as lithium, sodium and potassium.
  • Newlands arranged the elements by increasing atomic mass and found that every eighth element resembled the first, like the notes of music. He called this the law of octaves, but it worked only for the lighter elements.
  • Dmitri Mendeleev, a Russian chemist, made the great breakthrough in 1869. He arranged the 63 elements then known in order of atomic mass, in a table where similar elements fell in the same column. Boldly, he left gaps for elements not yet discovered and predicted their properties. When gallium, scandium and germanium were found later, they matched his predictions closely.

The modern periodic law

Mendeleev's table had a few elements out of place. In 1913 Henry Moseley showed that the true basis of an element is not its atomic mass but its atomic number, the number of protons. This gave the modern periodic law:

The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers. That is, when elements are arranged in order of atomic number, similar properties recur at regular intervals.

How the table is built

The modern table has 7 horizontal rows called periods and 18 vertical columns called groups.

  • Periods: all elements in a period have the same number of electron shells. Elements of period 2 have two shells; those of period 3 have three. The first period has only 2 elements, hydrogen and helium; the second and third have 8 each.
  • Groups: elements in a group have the same number of valence electrons and therefore similar chemical properties.

Some families to know

  • Group 1, alkali metals (lithium, sodium, potassium): soft, very reactive metals with one valence electron.
  • Group 2, alkaline earth metals (magnesium, calcium).
  • Group 17, halogens (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine): reactive non-metals with seven valence electrons.
  • Group 18, noble gases (helium, neon, argon): their outer shells are full, so they hardly react at all.

Metals lie on the left and in the middle of the table, non-metals on the right. Along a zig-zag line between them lie the metalloids, such as silicon, which show properties of both.

Trends in the table

  • Atomic size decreases from left to right across a period, because the growing charge of the nucleus pulls the electrons closer. It increases down a group, because a new shell is added at each step.
  • Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group.
  • Valency across a period first rises from 1 to 4 and then falls to 0.

The first twenty elements

Students are expected to know these in order: hydrogen, helium, lithium, beryllium, boron, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, neon, sodium, magnesium, aluminium, silicon, phosphorus, sulphur, chlorine, argon, potassium, calcium.

Key terms

Period
a horizontal row of the periodic table
Group
a vertical column of the periodic table
Periodic law
properties of elements recur regularly when arranged by atomic number
Halogens
the reactive non-metals of group 17
Noble gases
the unreactive gases of group 18
Metalloid
an element with properties of both metals and non-metals

Try it yourself

Make flash cards for the first twenty elements: name on one side; symbol, atomic number and electron arrangement on the other. Lay them out in the shape of the periodic table and say what the elements in each column have in common.

Quick questions

Answer in your head or on paper first, then tap to check.

State the modern periodic law.

The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers.

How many periods and groups does the modern periodic table have?

7 periods and 18 groups.

Why are noble gases unreactive?

Their outermost shells are completely filled.

How does atomic size change across a period, and why?

It decreases from left to right, because the increasing nuclear charge pulls the electrons closer.
